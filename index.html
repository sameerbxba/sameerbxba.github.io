ProcessServiceNowEquitable Life

Approval workflow redesign

Approvals were slow, and the room was asking how much to automate. Two weeks of shadowing showed that many were judgment calls the users didn't want automated, so I split approvals into rule-based and judgment-required and proposed routing rules for the first and a structured review queue for the second.

Why it worked: users adopted it without resistance, because they kept oversight exactly where it mattered to them. The data framed the choice, but the choice itself was about where people still needed to make the call.

Technology risk & AI governance
AI AgentControlsPython

An AI agent that reviews SOC 2 reports, and cannot act without permission

The agent reads a vendor's SOC 2 report, checks it against an organisation's own control requirements, and records one cited finding per requirement, holding the distinctions that matter: design versus operating effectiveness, tested evidence versus management's assertion, out of scope versus failed.

What matters more is what it can't do. Six controls sit between the model asking and the program acting, including human approval on every write and a citation check that rejects any finding whose quote isn't word for word in the source.

What it taught me: the approval step counted any input other than yes as a refusal, so a stray keystroke was logged as a human decision nobody made. That's worse than having no control at all, because it creates a fake record of oversight. I only found it by running the tool, and the fix is in the assessment.

GovernanceControlsPython

An attestation cycle that refuses to count what it cannot prove

Once a quarter, every application owner is asked to confirm their record is still accurate. It breaks in ways nobody notices: owners leave, replies aren't answers, records change after they were confirmed, and the response rate gets reported because it's the easy number. This is that cycle as code, with the failures made visible.

Eight controls decide what counts. A reply in prose, a colleague answering for the owner, or a reply after the deadline isn't an attestation, and every attestation is bound by digest to the exact record version the owner saw. The recorded quarter shows a 90 percent response rate and 23 percent coverage, and that gap is the point.

What it taught me: the first version contradicted its own headline claim. A reply arriving after expiry overwrote the "not attested" exception and counted as a full attestation, taking coverage to 100 percent. I built it with an AI coding assistant, then attacked it with AI-assisted adversarial review and mutation testing, which also showed five controls whose tests still passed with the control deleted. The failures and fixes are in the control matrix under their own heading.

Generative AIPersonal build2026

SyncBase

A tool that reads across a project's documents and flags where teams are working from different assumptions, the kind of mismatch that turns into rework three weeks later. I built it to test where generative AI fits into real work, and where it doesn't.

What it taught me: the model was the easy part. The hard part was deciding which of a long list of flags mattered. That's a judgment call, not a technical one. I chose precision over recall, because a tool that flags everything gets ignored on day two.

The live version is a front-end demo: projects are stored in your browser and the scanner shows pre-computed results, so it runs without an API key.

A NIST AI Risk Management Framework assessment of SyncBase, written the way I'd want one written about a system I had to sign off on. How I work, regardless of the title

Five co-op terms across four organisations turned out to be the same job in different settings. It usually comes down to four steps.

01 Understand it Sit with the people doing the work, because the documented version of a process and the real one are never quite the same.
02 Find the signal Use the data to find what matters, so the decision isn't a guess.
03 Make it usable Turn the finding into something clear enough to act on: a recommendation, a dashboard, or a decision someone can make.
04 Confirm it stuck Check whether it held after handover, because a change nobody adopts isn't finished.

04  /  About

Where this came from

I started an engineering degree expecting to spend it on hardware, and across five co-op terms found that the part I kept gravitating toward was the thinking around it: why a decision stalls, why the same problem keeps reappearing, and why a team of ten can't say where something stands. The common thread in everything below is translating between the people who have the problem, the people building the fix, and the leaders who have to decide.

Lately I've been going deeper on AI governance, which is the same question one layer down. When the thing sitting inside a workflow is a model rather than a rule, what is it deciding, who owns it, and how do we know it still works? I've worked through the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001 and OSFI's E-23 guidance on model risk, and instead of just reading them I applied them to two systems I built myself. Auditing your own work is uncomfortable in a useful way, and both assessments are on this page, including the parts where I found I'd been wrong.
