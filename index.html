About me

01 / Background

I started in Computer Engineering, switched to Electrical after second year, and somewhere across five co-op terms found that the part I kept gravitating toward wasn't the hardware. It was the delivery around it: why a commitment slips, why the same blocker keeps reappearing, why a team of ten can't say where a piece of work actually is.

That's taken me through project delivery, digital transformation, PMO coordination, and data analytics — in insurance, shipbuilding, a Crown corporation, and an early-stage AI startup. Project intake and tracking in JIRA, RAID logs and status reporting, requirements and documentation, training and UAT through to release. I'm most useful in the seat between delivery teams, business stakeholders, and leadership, translating between them so the work actually lands.

Lately I've been going deeper on AI governance, which is the same delivery question one layer down: when the thing inside a workflow is a model rather than a rule, what is it deciding, who owns it, and how do we know it still works. I'm working through the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, and OSFI's E-23 guidance on model risk.

SyncBase

A tool that reads across a project's documents and flags where teams are working from different assumptions — the kind of quiet mismatch that turns into rework three weeks later. Built to test my own thinking about where GenAI genuinely fits in operations work.

What it taught me: the model was the easy part. The hard part was deciding which of fifty flags were the three that mattered. Precision over recall, because a tool that flags everything gets ignored.

ServiceNowWorkflow redesignEquitable Life

Approval workflow redesign

Approvals were slow and the conversation was about how much to automate. Two weeks of shadowing showed that a meaningful share involved judgment calls users didn't want automated away. So I categorized approvals into rule-based versus judgment with rough volumes on each, and proposed the split: routing rules for the rule-based ones, a structured review queue for the rest.

Why it worked: the users adopted it without resistance, because they kept oversight exactly where it mattered to them.

Warranty management build, discovery to go-live

Seven departments, each with its own intake process, and nobody able to see the whole flow. I ran discovery across all seven, mapped case, warranty, approval, and escalation end to end, split it into user stories with acceptance criteria, and ran the sprint cadence in JIRA through UAT and regression to a launch with zero critical defects.

The real result: teams who only knew their own step started working from one shared picture — and when my lead was away, the cadence, backlog, and decision log kept running.
