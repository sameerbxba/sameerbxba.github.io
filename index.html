1.1

An AI agent that reviews SOC 2 reports, and cannot act without permission

A system that takes actions rather than just producing answers, and the six controls standing between it and anything it writes.

AI agent  /  Controls  /  Python  /  2026

The agent reads a vendor's SOC 2 report, assesses it against a receiving organisation's own control requirements, and records one cited finding per requirement. It has to hold the distinctions that matter in that work: design versus operating effectiveness, tested evidence versus management's assertion, out of scope versus failed, and the vendor's control versus yours.

The interesting part is not that it reads the report. It is what it is stopped from doing. Six controls sit between the model asking to do something and the program doing it, including a per-action human approval on anything that writes, and a citation check that rejects a finding unless the quoted evidence appears word for word in the source.

What it taught me: the approval step had a defect where any input other than yes counted as a refusal, so a stray keystroke was written into the audit log as a human decision nobody made. A control that manufactures false evidence of oversight is worse than no control, because the evidence is what everyone downstream relies on. I found it by running the tool rather than by reading it, and the fix and the reasoning are both in the assessment.

1.2

Reconciliation, from two hours to ten minutes

Automating the checks meant the reporting stopped depending on who happened to run it that week.

Data  /  SQL & Python  /  Ontario Lottery and Gaming

I used SQL and Python to match financial, transaction and vendor records across systems, surfacing discrepancies and missing approvals that nobody had the time to catch by hand. I then automated four recurring reconciliation and reporting workflows around those checks.

The result: the clearest of the four took about two hours manually and ran in roughly ten minutes afterwards, and the reporting became more consistent because the process stopped depending on who happened to run it. I also built the Tableau and Power BI views leadership used weekly, designed around the decision each audience actually had to make.

1.3

SyncBase

The model turned out to be the easy part, and deciding which flags were worth surfacing was the whole problem.

Generative AI  /  Personal build  /  2026

A tool that reads across a project's documents and flags where teams are working from different assumptions, the kind of quiet mismatch that turns into rework three weeks later. I built it to test my own thinking about where generative AI genuinely fits into real work, and where it does not.

What it taught me: the model was the easy part. The hard part was deciding which of a long list of flags were the few that actually mattered, and that is a judgment call rather than a technical one. I chose precision over recall, because a tool that flags everything gets ignored on day two.

1.4

Approval workflow redesign

Why I recommended against automating the part of the process I had specifically been asked to automate.

Process  /  ServiceNow  /  Equitable Life of Canada

Approvals were slow, and the conversation in the room was about how much of the process to automate. Two weeks of shadowing showed that a meaningful share of them involved judgment calls the users did not want automated away, so I categorised approvals into rule-based and judgment-required, put rough volumes against each, and proposed the split: routing rules for the first group, a structured review queue for the second.

Why it worked: users adopted it without resistance, because they kept oversight exactly where it mattered to them. The lesson that stuck is that the analysis was the easy part, and getting the human judgment call right was the whole game.

1.5

Governing my own AI tool

What it is like to assess your own tool properly, and to find a control you had documented as implemented but never actually built.

AI governance  /  NIST AI RMF  /  2026

A NIST AI Risk Management Framework assessment of SyncBase, written the way I would want one written about a system I had to sign off on. It separates the one genuinely AI feature from the rule-based one, because governing string concatenation as though it were a model is how these documents lose credibility. It covers eight risks with likelihood, impact and named treatments, a model card, a monitoring plan, and the numeric thresholds at which I would switch the scanner off.

The section I would point to: the governance decision on precision over recall, where the document states what that choice costs rather than only what it buys. Verifying the assessment against my own source code then turned up a control I had marked as implemented that did not exist. The correction and the fix are both in the change log, because a governance document that was never wrong usually means nobody checked.

1.6

Warranty build, discovery to go-live

Getting seven teams who could each see only their own step to work from a single picture of the whole flow.

Delivery  /  Salesforce  /  Seaspan

Seven departments, each with its own intake process, and nobody able to see the whole flow. I ran discovery across all seven, mapped case, warranty, approval and escalation end to end, split that into the requirements the Salesforce build ran on, and coordinated testing through to a launch with zero critical defects.

The real result: teams who only knew their own step started working from one shared picture, and when my lead was away the cadence, the backlog and the decision log all kept running without escalation.

How I work, regardless of the title

Five co-op terms across four organisations turned out to be the same job wearing different clothes. These four steps are what that job actually consists of.

2.1 Understand it Sit with the people doing the work, because the documented version of a process and the real one are never quite the same.
2.2 Find the signal Cut through volume to what actually matters, using data and evidence rather than impressions, so that the decision is not a guess.
2.3 Make it usable Turn the finding into something clear enough to act on: a recommendation, a dashboard, or a decision somebody is able to make.
2.4 Confirm it stuck Check whether it held after handover, because an insight nobody acts on, or a change nobody adopts, was never really finished.

4.0

Where this came from

I started an engineering degree expecting to spend it on hardware, and across five co-op terms found that the part I kept gravitating toward was the thinking around it: why a decision stalls, why the same problem keeps reappearing, and why a team of ten cannot say where something actually stands. The common thread in everything above is translating between the people who have the problem, the people building the fix, and the leaders who have to decide.

Lately I have been going deeper on AI governance, which is the same question one layer down. When the thing sitting inside a workflow is a model rather than a rule, what is it deciding, who owns it, and how do we know it still works. I have worked through the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001 and OSFI's E-23 guidance on model risk, and rather than stop at reading them I applied them to two systems I built myself. Auditing your own work is uncomfortable in a useful way, and both assessments are on this page, including the parts where I found I had been wrong.
