About

I started in Computer Engineering, switched to Electrical after second year, and across five co-op terms found the part I kept gravitating toward wasn't the hardware. It was the thinking around it: why a decision stalls, why the same problem keeps reappearing, why a team of ten can't say where something actually stands.

That's taken me through strategy and transformation consulting, data analytics, and project delivery, in insurance, shipbuilding, a Crown corporation, and an early-stage AI company. SQL and Python on messy operational data, requirements and process work, dashboards leadership actually used, and the reporting and coordination that keeps complex work moving. The common thread is translating between the people who have the problem, the people building the fix, and the leaders who have to decide.

Lately I've been going deeper on AI governance, which is the same question one layer down: when the thing inside a workflow is a model rather than a rule, what is it deciding, who owns it, and how do we know it still works. I've worked through the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, and OSFI's E-23 guidance on model risk, and rather than stop at reading them I applied the NIST framework to a tool I'd built myself, assessment here. Auditing your own work is uncomfortable in a useful way.

Generative AIPersonal build2026

SyncBase

A tool that reads across a project's documents and flags where teams are working from different assumptions, the kind of quiet mismatch that turns into rework three weeks later. I built it to test my own thinking about where GenAI genuinely fits in real work.

What it taught me: the model was the easy part. The hard part was deciding which of fifty flags were the three that mattered. Precision over recall, because a tool that flags everything gets ignored on day two. The real skill wasn't the AI, it was the judgment about what's worth surfacing.

I also wrote the governance documentation for it, against the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Writing it is what caught a control I had documented as implemented and hadn't actually built.

AI GovernanceNIST AI RMF2026

Governing my own AI tool

A NIST AI Risk Management Framework assessment of SyncBase, written the way I'd want one written about a system I had to sign off on. It separates the one genuinely AI feature from the rule-based one, because governing string concatenation as if it were a model is how these documents lose credibility. Eight risks with likelihood, impact and named treatments, a model card, a monitoring plan, and the numeric thresholds at which I'd switch the scanner off.

The section I'd point to: the governance decision on precision over recall. The scanner deliberately surfaces fewer conflicts than it could, and the document states what that choice costs, not just what it buys. Verifying it against my own source code turned up a control I'd marked as implemented that didn't exist. The correction and the fix are both in the change log, because a governance document that was never wrong usually means nobody checked.

Reconciliation, from two hours to ten minutes

At Ontario Lottery and Gaming I used SQL and Python to match financial, transaction, and vendor records across systems, surfacing discrepancies and missing approvals nobody had time to catch by hand. Then I automated four recurring reconciliation and reporting workflows.

ProcessServiceNowEquitable Life

Approval workflow redesign

Approvals were slow and the conversation was about how much to automate. Two weeks of shadowing showed that a meaningful share involved judgment calls users didn't want automated away. So I categorized approvals into rule-based versus judgment, with rough volumes on each, and proposed the split: routing rules for the rule-based ones, a structured review queue for the rest.

Why it worked: users adopted it without resistance, because they kept oversight exactly where it mattered to them. The lesson that stuck: the analysis was easy, getting the human judgment call right was the whole game.
