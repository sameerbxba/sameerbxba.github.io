The approval workflow that did not need automating

The brief was how much of the approval process to automate. The answer turned out to be less than anybody expected, and the delay was not where the question assumed it was.

ContextEquitable Life of Canada
My roleStrategy & digital transformation co-op
WhenSep – Dec 2025
Built withServiceNow, Power BI, process mapping
01  /  The situation

A brief that arrived with the answer already in it

Approvals were slow, and the question in the room when I arrived was how much of the approval process to automate. That framing was the brief, and the deliverable implied by it was a plan for how much could be taken out of human hands.

It is a reasonable-sounding question. It is also a question that already contains its own answer, which is the kind I have learned to be careful with.

02  /  What I actually did

Two weeks of watching before proposing anything

Rather than start from the automation question, I spent the first stretch of the project shadowing the people doing the approvals and reading what actually moved through the process.

Three things came out of that:

  • A meaningful share of approvals involved judgment that the approvers did not want automated away. These were not slow because of process friction. They were slow because somebody was thinking.
  • The delay was concentrated at a handoff that nobody owned, rather than being spread across approvers. Work would sit between two teams because it was not anybody's to pick up.
  • The two groups of approvals behaved completely differently and were being measured as one number, which is why the problem had looked like general slowness.

So I categorised approvals into rule-based and judgment-required, put rough volumes against each, and proposed a split rather than a percentage: routing rules for the first group, and a structured review queue for the second. I built the business case around the handoff rather than around approver speed, and built the Power BI reporting that turned a stuck sign-off from an administrative item into a visible schedule risk.

03  /  The decision that mattered

Not automating the part I had been asked to automate

The judgment-required approvals were the ones with the most apparent time in them, and therefore the most attractive to automate. I recommended leaving them with people, and putting the effort into making the queue structured and visible instead.

The reasoning

Automating a judgment call does not remove the judgment. It relocates it to somebody who has less context, or it gets routed around by people who know the decision matters and quietly go back to the old path. Either way the control looks like it is operating and is not.

The measurable outcome was a reduction in handoff delays of roughly a quarter. The outcome I would actually point to is that the change was adopted without resistance, because the people doing the work kept oversight exactly where it mattered to them.

04  /  What I got wrong

I worked inside the brief before I questioned it

For the first part of the project I took the framing at face value and worked on the question I had been handed, which was how much to automate. The useful work only started once I stopped answering that question and went to watch what was actually happening.

Two weeks is not a disaster on a four-month term, and discovery was what corrected it. But the correction came from a habit rather than from a decision, and I would rather it had been deliberate. The specific mistake was treating the stated problem as the problem, when the stated problem was a proposed solution wearing a question mark.

What I do differently now

I try to separate, out loud and early, the problem somebody is experiencing from the solution already implied in how they described it. "Approvals are slow" and "how much should we automate" are not the same brief, and only one of them is a problem statement.

Asking that at the start costs one awkward conversation. Not asking it costs two weeks, and sometimes costs the whole project.

05  /  What I would do differently

Mostly about evidence

  • Baseline properly before changing anything. The quarter reduction is a real improvement, but the measurement around it was lighter than I would want if somebody asked me to defend the number rather than report it.
  • Get the handoff formally owned. The delay sat at a point that belonged to nobody, and the fix relied on visibility rather than on a named owner. Visibility works until attention moves elsewhere.
  • Write down the judgment criteria. The split between rule-based and judgment-required was sound, but it lived in the analysis rather than in a document the team would maintain. Categories like that decay quietly.

The thread from here to the AI governance work is more direct than it looks. The question in both is the same: when part of a process stops being a person and becomes a rule or a model, what is actually deciding, who owns it, and how would anyone know if it stopped working.
