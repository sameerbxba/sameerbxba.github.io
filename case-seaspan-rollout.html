Case study  ·  Delivery & enablement

Seven departments, one warranty process, and a launch people could use

Warranty cases moved through seven departments that could each see only their own step. The job was to get all of them onto one Salesforce Service Cloud process, and then make sure going live wasn't where the work stopped.

ContextSeaspan ULC, North Vancouver
My roleDigital solutions & PM co-op, in the PMO
WhenJan – Apr 2025
Worked withSalesforce Service Cloud, BPMN, Smartsheet, Tempus
01  /  The situation

A process nobody could see from end to end

A warranty case at a shipyard doesn't belong to one team. It gets logged, assessed, approved, sometimes escalated, and closed, and each of those steps sat with a different department. Seven of them, each with its own intake process and its own idea of what the case was.

Nobody was doing their step badly. The problem was that nobody could see the whole flow, so a case could stall between two teams and neither would know it was theirs. Seaspan was moving warranty case management onto Salesforce Service Cloud, and I joined the PMO for the term that took it from discovery to go-live.

02  /  What I did

Discovery first, then requirements the build could run on

The build itself was the development team's work. Mine was everything around it that decides whether a build lands:

  • Discovery across all seven departments. I sat with each one to learn how a case really moved through their step, which wasn't always how it was documented.
  • One end-to-end map. I mapped case, warranty, approval and escalation in BPMN, so each team could see the steps on either side of their own for the first time.
  • Requirements from the map. I split that flow into the requirements the build ran on, across five two-week sprints.
  • Running the cadence. I ran the sprint ceremonies and made sure every open blocker had a named owner and a next step before the next meeting, so nothing sat around in between.
  • Testing and release readiness, coordinated through to a launch with zero critical defects.
03  /  The decision that mattered

Asking what decision each request was for

The rollout wasn't the only thing on my plate. The same term I was keeping Smartsheet project plans current for leadership, building Tempus reporting for resource managers, and answering ad hoc asks from executives trying to make quick calls. Four audiences, and every one of them asked for something slightly different from what they needed.

The habit that made it manageable was stepping back on every request to ask what business decision it was meant to support. A resource manager asking for a report usually needed an answer to one question about next month. An executive asking for status usually needed to know whether to step in.

The principle

An update nobody can act on is noise. I tried to turn every vague ask into a one-line takeaway with an owner and a next step, because that's something a person can make a decision from.

04  /  After go-live

Going live wasn't the finish line

If people work around a new system after launch, it hasn't really launched. So the last part of the term went on the people using it: I wrote the end-user training and job aids for the new process, so that someone who only knew their own step could work in the new one without having to ask.

I also built the resource and capacity reporting that managers used directly for planning decisions. I hadn't used Smartsheet or Tempus before that term, so the first stretch was learning what the people reading those reports used them to decide, and building backwards from that.

The defect count was fine, but the result I'd point to is that teams who only knew their own step started working from one shared picture, and when my lead was away the cadence, the backlog and the decision log all kept running without escalation.

05  /  What I got wrong

I held the map back until it felt finished

The end-to-end map was the most useful thing I produced that term, and I sat on it for longer than I should have. I wanted it complete before anyone saw it, so I kept refining the flow on my own instead of putting a rough version in front of the seven departments early.

When they did see it, the corrections came quickly: a handoff drawn in the wrong place, an approval step one team had never mentioned because to them it was obvious. None of it was hard to fix. All of it would have surfaced sooner with a rougher draft, and the requirements that depended on it would have started from a truer picture.

What I do differently now

I share the first rough version early now. A map is really a question for the people who do the work, and the fastest way to get it right is to let them mark it up while it's still cheap to change.

06  /  What I took from it

A rollout lands when people use it

  • Discovery is what makes requirements true. The documented process and the real one were never quite the same, and the requirements were only as good as the time spent watching.
  • Every blocker needs a name next to it. Most delays on the project weren't hard problems, just ones nobody had been asked to own.
  • Enablement is part of delivery. Training and job aids decide whether people use the new process or go back to the old one.

This was a co-op term in Seaspan's PMO. The same term included the Smartsheet and Tempus reporting described above.
